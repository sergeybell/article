\begin{abstract}
A novel experimental setup with spatially separated magnetic and electrostatic fields is proposed for studying the electric dipole moment (EDM) of deuterons and protons. The design leverages Wien filters to achieve precise measurements under "quasi-frozen" spin conditions, demonstrating adaptability to existing facilities for research at higher energy levels.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{Wien filter, Electrostatic deflector, Quasi-frozen spin, Electric dipole moment}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec.I}
\par The investigation of the electric dipole moment (EDM) of fundamental particles, such as protons and deuterons, constitutes a critical endeavor in contemporary physics. EDM offers an unparalleled opportunity to probe CP violation and validate theoretical models that describe particle interactions beyond the framework of  the Standard Model. For charged particles, the precise measurement of EDM necessitates the use of accelerators and the development of novel methodologies and technologies, requiring the adaptation of existing accelerator facilities to meet these experimental demands.

\par This paper explores a versatile structure characterized by spatially segregated magnetic and electrostatic fields, uniquely designed to facilitate the study of EDM in both deuterons and protons. A notable feature of this structure is its ability to support experiments for both types of particles, thereby enhancing its applicability and efficiency. Special emphasis is placed on the utilization of Wien filters to compensate for the magnetic dipole moment (MDM) and establish conditions conducive to a "quasi-frozen" spin. The analysis elucidates how such a structure can be adapted for facilities not originally intended for such experiments and explores its potential for advancing research at higher energy levels.

\par For an ansamble of particles, spin describes as a vector and evaluate in external EM-fields as a classical value with T-BMT equation \cite{b1}

\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned}
		\dv{\vec{S}}{t} = & \left(\vec{\Omega}_{\text{MDM}}+\vec{\Omega}_{\text{EDM}}\right)\times\vec{S}, \\
		\vec{\Omega}_{\text{MDM}}  = & - \frac{q}{m \gamma}\left\{(\gamma G+1) \vec{B}_{\perp}+(G+1) \vec{B}_{\|}-\right. \\
								     & \left.-\left(\gamma G+\frac{\gamma}{\gamma+1}\right) \frac{\vec{\beta} \times \vec{E}}{c}\right\}, \\

\end{equation}

\subsection{Relative spin motion at QFS}    

For QFS we should first maintain close orbit condition

\begin{equation}
	\Phi_p^{\textrm{arc}}+\Phi_{p}^{\textrm{comp}}=\frac{2\pi}{N}\ \ \
	\label{eq:QFS_orbital}
\end{equation}

\noindent where index $p$ states for momentum, and $\Phi_p^{\textrm{arc}}$ and $\Phi_{p}^{\textrm{comp}}$ -- total momentum rotation in arc and spin compensator element, $N$ is the lattice periodicity.

\par The second condition is to compensate MDM spin rotation in ring 

\begin{equation}
	\Phi_s^{\textrm{arc}}+\Phi_{s}^{\textrm{comp}}=0\ \ \
	\label{eq:QFS_spin}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\Phi_s^{\textrm{arc}}$ and $\Phi_{s}^{\textrm{comp}}$ -- total spin rotation in arc and spin compensator.
\par  If we consider a ring with $\Phi_p^{\textrm{arc}} = \frac{2\pi}{N}$, then $\Phi_{p}^{\textrm{comp}} = 0$, so it should not violate orbit at all. From the eq. \ref{eq:QFS_orbital} for spin compensation element with both magnetic and electric 

\begin{equation}
	\Phi_{p\textrm{B}}^{\textrm{comp}}+{\Phi}_{p\textrm{E}}^{\textrm{comp}} = 0\ \ \ 
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\Phi_{p\textrm{B}}^{\textrm{comp}}$ and ${\Phi}_{p\textrm{E}}^{\textrm{comp}}$ -- total momentum rotation in magnetic and electric field at spin compensator.

The ratio between orbital and spin rotation as follows given by spin-tune in magnetic field $\Phi_{s}^{\text{arc}} = \nu_{\text{B}}\Phi_{p}^{\text{arc}}$, $\Phi_{sB}^{\text{comp}} = \nu_{\text{B}}\Phi_{p\text{B}}^{\text{comp}}$ and electrical $\Phi_{s\text{E}}^{\text{comp}} = \nu_{\text{E}}\Phi_{p\text{E}}^{\text{comp}}$

Finally, for rotation via magnetic field in spin compensator element

\begin{equation}
	\Phi_{p\textrm{B}}^{\textrm{comp}} = -\Phi_{p\textrm{E}}^{\textrm{comp}} = -{\Phi}_{p}^{\text{arc}}\frac{2\pi}{N}\frac{\gamma^2 G}{G+1}\ \ \ 
	\label{eq:deflection}
\end{equation}

\par No mention was made of the physical structure of the compensation element, only of the integral features of the presented field components. So, the principles described here can be applied to both a Wien filters and an electrostatic deflectors with magnetic kickers.

\subsection{Spin compensation element length}

Spin compensation element length can be calculated for magnetic and electric field

\begin{equation}
	L = \Phi_{p\text{B}}^{\text{comp}}R_{\text{B}} = \Phi_{p\text{E}}^{\text{comp}}R_{\text{E}}
	\label{eq:length}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $R_{B}, R_{E}$ -- radius of magnetic and electric field. In general, total radius for element with combined magnetic and electric fields can be find as

\begin{equation}
	\frac{1}{R} = \frac{1}{R_{\text{B}}}+\frac{1}{R_{\text{E}}}
	\label{eq:total_radius}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $B\rho = \cfrac{p_{0}}{e}$ -- magnetic rigidity, $p_{0} = \gamma m\beta c$, $\kappa = \cfrac{p_{0}\beta c}{e}$ -- electrical rigidity. If we consider no orbital distortion in total, Lorentz force should be zero, so $R=0$ and $R_{B} = -R_{E}$.

The limitation on the strength of the electric field is much more significant than in the case of a magnetic field. According to the eqs. \ref{eq:deflection} and \ref{eq:length}

\begin{equation}
	L_{\text{min}} = \Phi_{p\text{E}}^{\text{comp}}R_{\text{E}} = - \frac{2\pi}{N}\frac{\gamma^2 G}{G+1}\frac{\kappa}{E_{\text{max}}}
	\label{eq:length_min}
\end{equation}
